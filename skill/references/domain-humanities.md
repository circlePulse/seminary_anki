# Domain Pack — Humanities / Social Science

Read alongside `card-design.md`. This covers what is different about a course whose
content is arguments rather than rules.

## Never card a contested claim as a fact

The single most damaging mistake in a humanities deck. A lecture says *Weber's
conceptualisations miss the mark*, or *Confucianism is far less prevalent than western
scholars assume*. Carded bare, that becomes a fact in your memory — and it is a
position, held by someone, against someone.

**Every card carrying a position names whose it is.**

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| How prevalent is Confucianism in East Asia? → Far less than assumed | On the reading's account, what do surveys show about Confucian self-identification, against what western scholars assume? → Very few self-identify, though Weber and others treat it as widespread |
| Is Confucianism a religion? → No | How does the Chinese state classify Confucianism and Daoism? → Confucianism not a religion, Daoism a religion |

The cost of getting this wrong shows up in essays, where reproducing a contested claim
as settled is exactly what loses marks.

## Decompose arguments into four parts

A paragraph of argument is never one card. Pull apart:

1. **The claim** — what is being asserted
2. **The ground** — the evidence or reasoning offered
3. **The holder** — whose position it is, or which text it comes from
4. **The target** — what it argues against, where the lecture says

Part 4 is the one routinely lost, and it is often the most examinable: positions in
the humanities are defined by what they reject.

## Definitional disputes are the content, not the preamble

When a course spends a lecture on "what is religion," that argument *is* the syllabus
— the rest of the course is applications of it. Card the competing criteria, who
proposes them, and the cases that break them. Do not compress it into one
definition card.

The same goes for any term the course flags as translated, imported, or contested.
Where a lecture says a concept doesn't travel between languages or cultures, that
observation is a card.

## Interference: many thinkers, similar positions

Structurally identical to the narrator problem in ḥadīth. Weber, Bellah, and a dozen
others will collapse into "some scholar said something about religion and economics"
unless each front carries distinguishing content.

- **Anchor every attribution.** Not "who wrote about religion and capitalism" but
  "who first argued for a link between Protestantism and modern capitalism, and on
  what is he judged to have missed the mark?"
- **Card the contrast where two thinkers are compared**, rather than two separate
  cards that differ only in a name.
- **Named concepts get their originator and date on the same note** — Sheilaism,
  Bellah, 1985. A concept without its source is unusable in an essay.

## Dates, periods, places

Same rule as elsewhere: a bare year is an orphan. Attach it to the thing it explains
— "Christianity reached Korea in the 1700s, and was first treated as heretical and
persecuted" rather than a date on its own.

## Quotations

Card sparingly, and as completion prompts, never as "what did X say about Y" — that
gives away the answer. Reserve them for phrasing you would actually need to quote.

## Tiering

- **Tier 1** — definitions and criteria, named concepts with their originators,
  contested positions with their holders, the structural claims a lecture returns to
- **Tier 2** — supporting statistics, illustrative cases, a scholar mentioned once
- **Tier 3** — the lecture's own scaffolding ("we'll come back to this")

**Careful with Tier 3 here.** A methodological commitment stated by the lecturer —
*we are going to be flexible about what counts as a religion*, *keep the difference
between doctrine and practice in mind* — sounds like scaffolding and is often exactly
what an exam question is built on. Tier 2 it rather than dropping it.
