## Corrections and flags

1. Teeth: your notes have "1 premolar" and "3 molars". Humans have 2 premolars and 3 molars on each side of each jaw (counting the wisdom tooth), and the Arabic names split them differently: al-ḍawāḥik is only the first premolar; al-ṭawāḥin is the second premolar plus the first two molars; al-nawājidh is the third molar. Your counts (1+1+1+1+3+1 = 8 per side) are right; only the English labels were off. The cards use the corrected English.
2. Soft palate: your "arrikhuw?" is al-ḥanak al-rikhw (the soft palate). It is also called al-ḥanak al-layyin; I kept that off the card so it can't act as a hint.
3. Hard palate: your "alsalbu". I vowelled it al-ṣulb (hard), the standard form. If your teacher says al-ṣalb, tell me and I'll match it.
4. Nasal cavity: the notes give no Arabic, so I added al-khayshūm, the standard tajwīd term.
5. Main areas: your notes list four (the jawf, throat, mouth and nasal cavity). Ibn al-Jazarī counts five, splitting the mouth into the tongue and the lips. That's a different grouping, not a mistake, but you'll meet the five-count in the Jazariyyah, so hafs-011 covers it.
6. Ṭaraf al-lisān: I used the English "blade" for your "tip (not actually the tip)". That's the phonetics term for the part just behind the tip.
7. Ikhtilās and ishmām: your teacher uses both as names of mistakes, and the cards follow that. Both are also names of sanctioned techniques. In Ḥafṣ, the word taʾmannā (Yūsuf 12:11) is read with ishmām or with rawm (ikhtilās). That isn't carded now.
8. Typo: "human air" should be "human ear".
9. Additions of mine, so you can check them:
   - The dots credited to Naṣr ibn ʿĀṣim and Yaḥyā ibn Yaʿmar (hafs-003).
   - The terms muḥaqqaq and muqaddar (hafs-039, hafs-040).
   - Ibn Sīnā's qarʿ (striking) and qalʿ (pulling apart) (hafs-038, hafs-041, hafs-042).
   - Madd ṭabīʿī (hafs-047).
   - Taqlīl versus imālah, and Ḥafṣ's one imālah in Hūd 11:41 (hafs-053).
   - The definition of the līn letters (hafs-043).
   - The example words.
   - The hearing range falling with age (hafs-037).
   - Heaviness coming from the back of the tongue (hafs-050).
10. One fact per card: "tongue down" and "jaw open" for alif are separate cards; wāw and yāʾ are split the same way.

- **hafs-001**
  - Front: How many letters does tajwīd count in the Arabic alphabet?
  - Back: 29 — the common count of 28 treats the hamzah and the alif as one letter; tajwīd counts them apart.
- **hafs-002**
  - Front: On what basis did the scholars arrange the Arabic letters in their alphabetical order?
  - Back: By similarity of written shape, so letters that look alike sit together: ب ت ث, ج ح خ, د ذ.
- **hafs-003**
  - Front: How did the scholars tell apart letters that share one written shape, such as ب ت ث?
  - Back: They added dots (iʿjām). The step is credited to Naṣr ibn ʿĀṣim and Yaḥyā ibn Yaʿmar, in the 1st century AH.
- **hafs-004**
  - Front: The first letter of the alphabet is written as an alif. Which letter is it really?
  - Back: The hamzah. The true alif is the madd alif, which can never begin a word; old alphabet lists show it inside لا (lām-alif).
- **hafs-005** (science label: tajwīd)
  - Front: What vowel is on the letter before a madd alif?
  - Back: Always a fatḥah. The alif itself is always sākin; it never occurs any other way.

  - Front: Between what frequencies can the human ear perceive sound?
  - Back: About 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz (20 kHz). The top of the range falls with age.
- **hafs-038** (cloze)
  - Text: Sound arises in four ways: {{c1::collision}} of two objects (a clap); {{c2::separation}} of two objects held together by cohesion (snapping a twig); {{c3::vibration}} of an object (a plucked string); {{c4::friction}} of one object against another (rubbing hands).
  - Extra: Ibn Sīnā names the first two as the causes of speech sounds: qarʿ (striking) and qalʿ (pulling apart).
- **hafs-039** (science label: tajwīd)
  - Front: How is a letter (ḥarf) defined in tajwīd?
  - Back: A sound that relies on an articulation point, either specific (muḥaqqaq) or approximate (muqaddar).
- **hafs-040** (science label: tajwīd)
  - Front: In the definition of a letter, what makes an articulation point approximate (muqaddar) rather than specific?
  - Back: The sound hits no particular organ. This is true of the madd letters, which come from the jawf.
- **hafs-041** (science label: tajwīd)
  - Front: What physically produces a sākin letter?
  - Back: A collision (qarʿ) between two parts of the speech organs, as when the lips press together on a sākin bāʾ.
- **hafs-042** (science label: tajwīd)
  - Front: What physically produces a letter that carries a vowel (mutaḥarrik)?
  - Back: Two parts of the speech organs separating (qalʿ), together with the articulation of the vowel's own source: a fatḥah comes from the alif's, a ḍammah from the wāw's, a kasrah from the yāʾ's.
- **hafs-043** (science label: tajwīd)
  - Front: What physically produces the madd and līn letters?
  - Back: Vibration of the vocal cords, shaped by a mouth position particular to each letter; no two organs meet. (Līn letters are wāw or yāʾ sākinah after a fatḥah, as in خَوْف and بَيْت.)
- **hafs-044**
  - Front: Which letters originate in al-jawf?
  - Back: The madd letters: alif, wāw and yāʾ when they lengthen the vowel before them. [diagram]
- **hafs-045** (science label: tajwīd)
  - Front: When is a wāw a madd letter?
  - Back: When it is sākin and the letter before it has a ḍammah, as in يَقُولُ.
- **hafs-046** (science label: tajwīd)
  - Front: When is a yāʾ a madd letter?
  - Back: When it is sākin and the letter before it has a kasrah, as in قِيلَ.
- **hafs-047** (science label: tajwīd)
  - Front: How long is a madd letter held when nothing after it calls for more?
  - Back: 2 counts — the natural madd (madd ṭabīʿī).
- **hafs-048** (science label: tajwīd)
  - Front: How must the lengths of the fatḥah, kasrah and ḍammah compare?
  - Back: They must all be equal: every short vowel gets the same single count.
- **hafs-049** (science label: tajwīd)
  - Front: What is the recitation mistake called ikhtilās?
  - Back: Shortening a vowel: taking away part of its sound, so it comes out shorter than the others.
- **hafs-050** (science label: tajwīd)
  - Front: What is it called when the lips push forward on a heavy letter?
  - Back: Ishmām — a mistake. Heaviness comes from the back of the tongue rising, never from the lips.
- **hafs-051**
  - Front: Where must the tongue be for a madd alif?
  - Back: Down, so the sound passes from the jawf through the throat and mouth unobstructed. [diagram]
- **hafs-052**
  - Front: What must the jaw do for a madd alif?
  - Back: Open: the space between the upper and lower back teeth must be open. [diagram]
- **hafs-053** (science label: tajwīd)
  - Front: What happens to a madd alif if the tongue isn't down or the jaw isn't open?
  - Back: It takes on a kasrah sound: taqlīl (a slight lean toward yāʾ) or imālah (a full lean). Both are mistakes in Ḥafṣ, except the one imālah in مجراها (Hūd 11:41).
- **hafs-054**
  - Front: Where does the tongue go for a madd wāw?
  - Back: A little back, but the sound's path stays unobstructed. [diagram]
- **hafs-055**
  - Front: What shape do the lips take for a madd wāw?
  - Back: Rounded, like an O. (On heavy letters, by contrast, the lips must not move forward.)
- **hafs-056**
  - Front: What does the tongue do for a madd yāʾ?
  - Back: Its middle rises up and back while the front goes down, still leaving room for the sound. [diagram]
- **hafs-057**
  - Front: In a madd yāʾ the middle of the tongue rises. What keeps room for the sound?
  - Back: The lower jaw drops a little more.
- **hafs-058**
  - Front: How does the sound of all three madd letters travel from the vocal cords to the outside?
  - Back: Without spreading: it goes out as a straight, focused stream.
